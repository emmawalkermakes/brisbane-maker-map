One of the best things about Brisbane's handmade markets is that the person behind the table usually made what's on it. That means you can often ask for something that isn't on the table at all: a mug glazed in a particular colour, a necklace with initials, a print of a specific street, a quilt in a baby's nursery colours.

Commissioning a piece is not complicated, but it goes much more smoothly when you know what to ask. Here are the questions people have, in roughly the order they come up.

## Is it rude to ask a maker for a custom piece?

Not at all. Many makers list "custom orders" or "commissions" on their sign, website or social media. Some don't take them at all, especially in the busy weeks before Christmas, and that's fine too. The polite approach is simply to ask: "Do you take custom orders, and are you still taking them for Christmas?"

If a stall is busy, don't try to brief the whole project on the spot. Take their card, photograph their sign (with permission), or follow them on social media, and get in touch after the market.

## Is it too late to commission something for Christmas?

It depends entirely on the maker and the medium, so ask rather than assume. Some timelines are set by the process, not the person:

- **Ceramics** need drying, at least one firing, glazing and often a second firing. Even small pieces can take weeks from clay to collection. (If you'd like to make your own, our guide to [Creekside Pottery's Christmas workshop dates](/posts/creekside-pottery-christmas-workshops-2026/) works through the firing wait.)
- **Textiles, quilting and embroidery** are usually limited by hours of handwork.
- **Jewellery** may depend on sourcing stones, chain or findings.
- **Art prints and illustration** can be quicker once a design is agreed, but proof rounds and printing still take time.

Many makers set their own Christmas order cut-off. If they say it has passed, take that at face value. A good alternative is to buy a gift card or a piece they have in stock, and commission the custom item for a birthday or anniversary in the new year.

## What do I need to tell the maker?

A clear brief saves rounds of back and forth. Before you message, have answers to these:

1. **What's it for?** Who it's for, and the occasion.
2. **What do you love in their existing work?** Point to a specific piece, photo or post. "Like the blue bowl in your 3 November post, but bigger" is far easier to work with than "something blue".
3. **Size and practical use.** Will it go in a dishwasher? Be worn every day? Hang on a wall?
4. **Personal details.** Exact names, dates and spelling, written down rather than said aloud.
5. **Your budget.** Saying a range up front is helpful, not awkward. It lets the maker suggest what's realistic.
6. **Your deadline.** The date you actually need it, plus whether you'll collect or need it posted.

Keep the brief in writing (email or a message), and keep their replies. It protects both of you.

## Should I expect to pay a deposit?

Often, yes. Many makers ask for a deposit or full payment before starting a custom piece, because a personalised item usually can't be sold to anyone else if the order falls through. Ask how payment works, what happens if you need to cancel, and whether the deposit is refundable. Every maker sets their own terms, so read them and ask if they're unclear.

## How much creative freedom should I give?

More than you might think. You're commissioning this particular maker because you like their style, so give them room to use it. Be firm about the things that matter (names, size, colours to avoid, the practical use) and relaxed about the rest.

Handmade pieces also vary. A glaze can run differently in the kiln, hand-dyed yarn shifts between batches, and hand lettering isn't a font. Ask the maker what will vary naturally and decide whether you're comfortable with that before you commit.

## Will I see it before it's finished?

Ask. For text, names or illustrations, a proof or sketch is common and well worth checking. Read every name and date slowly, ideally with a second person. Once a name is fired into clay or stamped into metal, there's no fixing a typo.

For three-dimensional work, some makers send progress photos, while others prefer to show only the finished piece. Agree on what you'll see and when.

## What if it's not quite what I imagined?

Be specific and kind. If something is wrong against the agreed brief (a misspelt name, the wrong size), the maker will normally want to know. If it's a matter of taste ("I thought it'd be more green"), that's harder, which is exactly why the brief and proof stages matter.

This is also where your written messages are useful: they show what was agreed.

## Collection or postage?

If you can collect in person, at a market or by arrangement, you avoid postage risk. That is worth thinking about for fragile pieces in the Christmas rush. If it needs to be posted, ask how it will be packed, whether postage is extra, and whether it will be tracked. Leave time for Australia Post's Christmas cut-off dates.

## How do I find makers who take commissions?

- **Talk to them at markets.** BrisStyle and The Market Folk both run Christmas markets with local makers before Christmas; dates are in our [Brisbane Christmas markets list](/posts/brisbane-christmas-markets-2026/).
- **Look at maker directories.** BrisStyle has an online directory of its members for shopping between markets.
- **Check social media.** Many makers post when they open or close commission slots.

## A quick commission checklist

- Ask whether they're taking custom orders and what their Christmas cut-off is.
- Send a written brief: purpose, reference piece, size, use, names, budget, deadline.
- Confirm price, deposit, cancellation terms and timeline in writing.
- Approve a proof for any names or text.
- Agree on collection or postage, including packing.
- Say thank you, and tag or review them if they'd like that. For small makers, word of mouth matters.

Done well, a commission gives you a gift nobody else has, and gives a local maker a project they're proud of.

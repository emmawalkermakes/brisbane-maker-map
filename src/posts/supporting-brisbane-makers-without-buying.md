By mid-December, a lot of us have hit the end of the Christmas budget. That doesn't mean you have to stop backing the makers whose work you love.

Small makers live and die by attention as much as sales. A potter, jeweller or candle maker running a one-person business usually has no marketing department, so the things you do as a fan can genuinely move the needle. Here are twelve ways to help that cost nothing or very little.

## Online (5 minutes each)

### 1. Follow, and actually engage

Following a maker on Instagram or Facebook helps, but likes, saves and comments help more, because they signal to the platform that people care about what's posted. A genuine comment ("This glaze is beautiful, is it the same as the mug from last month?") also tells the maker what resonates.

### 2. Share their posts with a personal note

Sharing a post to your stories with a line like "I bought one of these at the markets and use it every day" is a personal recommendation, and personal recommendations are what small businesses run on.

### 3. Leave a review

If you've bought from a maker before, a short, specific review on Google, their website or their marketplace shop can help future customers decide. "Arrived well packed, exactly like the photos, the glaze is even nicer in person" is more useful than five stars with no words.

### 4. Join their mailing list

Email newsletters reach people directly, without depending on an algorithm. Signing up costs nothing and often gets you first notice of new collections, market dates and sales.

### 5. Tag them when you use their work

If you post a photo of your table setting, your earrings or a gift you received, tag the maker. It's free advertising that also happens to be true.

### 6. Turn up to markets anyway

A busy market is good for every stallholder. Foot traffic makes a market viable, keeps organisers running it, and draws more people in. Even if you're "just looking", you're part of the crowd that makes the day work. Our [market calendar](/markets/) lists upcoming dates confirmed with organisers. Check the organiser's page before you go.

### 7. Talk to the maker (when they're not busy)

Makers often enjoy explaining how something is made. Asking good questions — "How long does a piece like this take?" or "Where do you source your timber?" — is a nice moment for both of you, and you'll come away knowing whose work to recommend. Just read the room: step back when a customer is ready to pay.

### 8. Take a business card or flyer

Picking up a card and putting it on your fridge means you'll remember the name next time a birthday comes around, or when a friend asks, "Do you know anyone who makes...?"

### 9. Ask before you photograph

It's polite to ask before photographing a stall or a maker's work. Some makers are happy for you to share; others worry about designs being copied. Asking also starts a conversation, and you can offer to tag them.

### 10. Refer them for bigger jobs

Makers often rely on word of mouth for commissions and bulk orders, such as wedding favours, corporate gifts, or a café looking for local mugs. If someone in your workplace or circle is planning something like that, pass on a name. One referral can be worth more to a maker than a dozen small sales. (If you're thinking of commissioning a piece yourself, our guide to [commissioning a local maker](/posts/commissioning-a-brisbane-maker-before-christmas/) covers what to ask.)

### 11. Wishlist them for later

Bookmark a maker's shop and put specific pieces on a list for birthdays or next Christmas. Some people share these lists with family, which steers gift money towards local makers instead of chain stores.

### 12. Respect their work

This one is about what *not* to do:

- **Don't haggle as a habit.** A handmade price covers materials, hours of skilled time, market fees and much more. If something's out of budget, it's fine to say so and come back another time.
- **Don't ask them to make something "like" another maker's design.** It puts makers in an awkward position.
- **Don't copy designs to make at home and sell.** Being inspired is one thing; reproducing someone's work is another.

## Why it matters

Brisbane's market scene runs on small operators: many makers do markets on weekends and evenings alongside other work, or are building their craft into a full-time business one stall at a time. Every follow, review and recommendation helps them reach the next customer, and helps keep the markets themselves going, so there's still something special to discover next Christmas.

So if your wallet needs a rest this December, keep showing up anyway. It counts.

At a dedicated handmade market, you can usually relax. BrisStyle, for example, describes its Twilight Market in King George Square as selling 100% locally handmade goods. But many of Brisbane's general weekend markets mix makers with resellers, importers and bric-a-brac, and that's fine — there's room for all of it.

If your aim this Christmas is to spend with local makers, though, it helps to know how to tell the difference. This is a checklist, not an accusation: most of the time, simply asking will tell you everything.

## Before you go

- [ ] **Check how the market describes itself.** Look at the organiser's website. Words like "handmade", "makers", "locally designed" or "curated" signal a different market from "general" or "variety". Some organisers state a handmade-only rule; others don't.
- [ ] **Look up the stallholder list if it's published.** Many organisers share line-ups on their website or social media before the day. Clicking through to a few makers' own pages tells you a lot.
- [ ] **Note that "locally designed" and "handmade" aren't the same.** A designer might create a print or pattern locally and have it produced elsewhere. That's still supporting a local small business, but it's worth knowing what you're paying for.

## At the stall: what to look for

- [ ] **Variation between items.** Handmade work usually varies slightly: glaze drips, brushstrokes, stitch lengths, wood grain. Twenty absolutely identical pieces aren't proof of anything, but they're a reason to ask.
- [ ] **A coherent style.** A maker's table usually looks like one person's work, with a recognisable palette or technique. A table mixing many unrelated styles and materials is more likely to be curated stock.
- [ ] **Maker's marks and labels.** Look for a stamp on the base of ceramics, a sewn-in label, a signature on a print, or a care card with the maker's name.
- [ ] **Process clues.** Some makers bring photos of their studio, works in progress, or display a sample of the raw material. These are good signs.
- [ ] **Small-batch language.** "One-off", "limited run" or "this glaze is sold out" suggests things are made in batches.
- [ ] **Brand packaging.** Retail packaging with barcodes and a big-brand logo is a hint that the item is bought in, unless the maker uses packaging made for their own brand.

## Questions that are polite to ask

Makers are generally happy to talk about their work. Good opening questions:

- [ ] "Did you make these?"
- [ ] "How long does one of these take?"
- [ ] "Where's your studio?"
- [ ] "Do you do custom orders?"
- [ ] "Is this dishwasher safe?" or "How should I care for this?" — a maker will know the answer for their own materials.

If the answers are vague or the person says they're minding the stall for someone else, that's not necessarily a problem. Plenty of makers have a friend or family member help out. You can always ask for the maker's name and look them up later.

## Signs that need a second look

None of these on its own means anything. Together, they're worth a question:

- [ ] Identical items you've seen at several unrelated stalls, or online marketplaces.
- [ ] No name, brand or contact details anywhere on the stall.
- [ ] Prices that seem very low for the amount of handwork involved.
- [ ] Care instructions or labels in a style that doesn't match the stall.
- [ ] The person at the stall can't say anything about how it's made.

## After the market

- [ ] **Look up the maker's website or social media.** Studio posts and process photos are the clearest confirmation, and you'll know where to find them again.
- [ ] **Keep their card.** Many makers sell online between markets and take commissions.
- [ ] **Follow organisers who vet stalls.** If handmade matters to you, favour markets that say they handpick or jury their stallholders.

## Why it matters at Christmas

The weeks before Christmas are a big part of the year for many small makers, and buying from them keeps money in the local creative economy. It also gets you a better story to go with the gift: who made it, where, and how.

But be kind about it. Resellers and importers are small businesses too, and a general market with a mix of stalls is a perfectly good outing. The aim is to spend where you *intend* to, not to police anyone else's table.
